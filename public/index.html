The sovereign, open source AI inference stack.

Cairn (codename) is a sovereign, open source AI inference stack your organization installs and operates on infrastructure it controls. Your teams keep the coding agents and chat assistants they already use and connect them to a single OpenAI-compatible API. Behind that API sits a tested stack of open source components — model access, security, observability — every one swappable by published contract. Your compute. Your keys. Your stack.

[01]Why it matters — and why now

Intelligence is becoming a utility. Economies will depend on inference the way they depend on electricity.

Every utility followed the same shape: generation, distribution, consumption. Nobody runs a factory off a neighbor's extension cord — the middle layer is what makes the power usable at scale. Intelligence is arriving in that same shape, and its middle layer is the inference stack.

Source: Epoch AI, “How many AI agents could run on the AI chips shipped through 2027?”, October 2026. Assumes hardware is fully deployed and dedicated to agentic workloads.

The capacity for agents is arriving fast. Who gets to use it is an open question. Businesses of every size will draw on that capacity to power their agentic workers — and owning the middle layer is what keeps their supply resilient. That is where sovereignty is decided: not in the model, but in how inference is served, governed and observed.

It matters most to the organizations no one is building this for. Small and medium businesses are 99.7% of enterprises and 60% of employment in the OECD non-financial business sector (OECD, Small, Medium, Strong — 2013 data, published 2017). They should be able to adopt AI on their own terms — safely, easily, cost-effectively — and most will reach it through partners. Broad, sovereign adoption of open source AI will be won or lost there.

[02]The problem

The models are not the problem. Deploying and running them is.

Open-weight models are now excellent, free and one download away. The gap between “we have the weights” and “our teams ship on them every day” is an entire platform — and most organizations are being asked to build that platform themselves. Six challenges keep showing up:

  1. No stack, only stitching. Inference-stack work today is bespoke: every team re-derives gateway, serving, observability and security from scratch, and every integration is one-of-one.
  2. Borrowed infrastructure. A hosted API routes your prompts, your data and your dependency through someone else's computer. Their pricing, their terms, their availability window.
  3. Borrowed software can be revoked. Recent relicensing of Terraform, Redis and Elasticsearch showed that “open” is not a promise. Only software under an OSI-approved license keeps your right to run, study, modify and fork it — a right no single party can withdraw.
  4. Fragmentation. Models × serving engines × hardware is a matrix. The right engine depends on all three, and getting it wrong means hand-tuning you'll pay for in production.
  5. Flying blind. There is no standard way to see what inference costs, how fast it is, or how much of it your hardware can take. Security defaults are DIY, deployment by deployment.
  6. Skill and scale. Assembling and maintaining a stack is platform-team work — and by the OECD numbers above, almost no organization has one.

So the demand side keeps asking for the same three things:

Independence

Your data, your policies, your choices. Everything runs on infrastructure you control, with components you can inspect and replace.

Turnkey

Open source without the assembly. A tested stack built to install, upgrade and run from its documentation.

Resilient

Own your capacity. Inference on infrastructure you own or share, with predictable costs and no single-vendor lock-in.

[03]What Cairn is

An open source AI inference stack that your organization installs and operates on infrastructure it controls.

Your teams connect the tools they already use to one inference API. Behind it, Cairn integrates existing open source components into a cohesive stack — from model access to observability and security — on compute you own. It is a research collaboration between Mozilla and Mila, released under Apache 2.0.

To be explicit: Cairn does not train or sell models, and it is not a hosted service. It is the stack you run.

The method behind the stack:

Specify, test, select, integrate

We specify what each layer must do, test existing open source components against that specification, then select and integrate the best fit.

Swappable by contract

We publish the interface contracts each component must satisfy, so one conforming component can replace another without reworking the rest.

Composition, not creation

We write the glue between components rather than new ones — and publish specifications where no suitable open source component exists yet.

Bring your own harness

Teams keep the chat assistants and coding agents they already use, and point them at one OpenAI-compatible API on the organization's own compute.

[04]How it differs

Many organizations are building inference stacks. No one has defined what a stack should contain. Cairn starts with the definition.

How Cairn differs from other inference stack initiatives
Typical initiativeCairn

Replacing a componentRework the integration, or wait for the vendorSwap any conforming component; the published contract guarantees compatibility

Open standards enable portability. Open source ensures the portability lasts. Its incentive structure is the only one compatible with true sovereignty: no single party can withdraw access, change the terms or end support.

[05]Architecture

Contract-defined layers, integrated open source components. The stack is defined by its seams — and the seams belong to no vendor.

Cairn is the server side of an agentic setup. Your tools stay yours and sit above the demarcation line: one OpenAI-compatible API, one issued key. Below it, on infrastructure you control, the stack is organized into layers — with security, observability and the control plane cutting across all of them.

The contracts — Model Access

Read left to right: a harness calls the model gateway over the OpenAI-compatible API with a team key; the gateway calls the model server over the same API on the internal network; a recipe pins what runs, on which hardware, with which settings. Everything streams telemetry out.


 harness                (1)               (2)                (3)
 (your tools)  ---------------->  model gateway  ------>  model server  + recipe
 chat, coding,                   (Otari)                 (vLLM | SGLang)  (pinned config)
 knowledge work

 1  harness -> gateway    POST /v1/chat/completions, GET /v1/models
                          Bearer <team key>              lets the harness change
 2  gateway -> server     OpenAI-compatible API at model-server:8000/v1
                          Bearer <server key>            lets the gateway change
 3  recipe -> server      recipes/<hardware>/<model>/<publisher>/<server>/
                          images pinned by tag           lets hardware/model change
 4  all components -> observability: metrics, logs, traces -> OTLP

Swappable by contract, in practice

The contracts are easiest to see in what does not move when a component changes. Replacing the serving engine is one line:

 # .env -- swap the serving engine
 - MODEL_SERVER=vllm
 + MODEL_SERVER=sglang

 stays the same    model · /v1 endpoint · server key · port
 recipe absorbs    gpu-memory-utilization  ->  mem-fraction-static

Same story across the seams: switch cpu to nvidia and only the recipe's image and runtime change. Swapping the gateway (LiteLLM → Otari — still a prototype) leaves the edge proxy, model server and base URL in place; the harness takes a new key and model name. One setting, one contract.

[06]The Alpha release

Today: the Model Access layer end to end, security defaults and first-class observability, running on one Linux host.

One secure entry point

A single published port served by Caddy. Every request is authenticated with gateway-issued keys; nothing else in the stack publishes a port.

Model gateway

Otari issues and revokes API keys per team, inspects usage and ships a built-in playground. Works with any tool that speaks an OpenAI-compatible or Anthropic-compatible API: base URL, key, model ID. That is the entire integration.

Model serving with recipes

A recipe serves one model, with one engine, on one hardware target — images and settings pinned by tag. vLLM and SGLang included. No GPU? CPU recipes let you try the whole stack before spending any hardware budget.

Observability & security out of the box

A Grafana LGTM bundle (Loki, Grafana, Tempo, Mimir, OTel Collector) with inference dashboards: throughput, TTFT/ITL/TPOT/E2E, KV-cache, traces from proxy to model server, logs, host metrics — and GPU metrics (NVIDIA only). Core services run no-new-privileges on isolated networks; PostgreSQL requires SCRAM-SHA-256.

Quick start

 git clone https://github.com/opencairn-ai/cairn-hoodoo.git && cd cairn-hoodoo
 ./env-setup.sh          # generates .env with fresh secrets, picks a recipe
 ./model-selection.sh    # or pick interactively, any time
 docker compose up -d    # ready when model-server reports healthy

Four commands to a private inference API with keys, budgets and dashboards on your own metal. Full instructions in the README.

Known limitations — said plainly

  • Single-node only. Multi-node serving is out of scope for Alpha.
  • A few model recipes. Seven tested today: CPU, any NVIDIA GPU, and specific RTX 6000, GB10 and DGX A100 setups.
  • Breaking changes expected between Alpha releases: config files, the gateway and the recipe layout may change.
  • Linux host required with Docker Engine and Compose; NVIDIA GPUs need the driver and Container Toolkit.
  • Heavy first launch. The first docker compose up downloads images and model weights — give yourself disk headroom and an HF_TOKEN.
  • Optional exporters widen the surface. If you enable them, the node and DCGM exporters run on the host network (ports 9100 and 9400 by default) and the DCGM exporter needs elevated privileges. Decline them, or firewall them, if you don't need those dashboards; GPU metrics are NVIDIA-only either way.

Offer capacity on an open stack configured to your hardware.

Open source projects

Build components against each layer's published contract.

Four ways to participate

Build From Oct

Deliver or help co-define a layer component against the interface contracts.

Suited to organizations already building parts of such a stack, and to open source projects.

Deploy From Nov–Dec

Run the reference implementation as a beta deployment partner: onboarding in November, deployed by January, in production by July 2027.

Suited to institutions and public bodies with strong IT teams, mandated to offer sovereign, safe inference to their staff.

Host From Feb–Mar

Provide a reference or test environment for multi-node validation across a growing set of tested configurations.

Suited to compute providers.

Distribute From Apr

Offer the stack to the organizations you serve — a shared service you operate, or a configuration with your own models and policy defaults.

Suited to development agencies, integrators, and nonprofits supporting networks of organizations.
